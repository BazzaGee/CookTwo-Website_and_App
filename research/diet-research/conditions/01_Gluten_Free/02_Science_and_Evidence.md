# Gluten-Free Diet — Science & Evidence

## Coeliac disease

- **Prevalence:** approximately 1% of the global population; higher in people with type 1 diabetes, autoimmune thyroid disease, Down syndrome and first-degree relatives (10–15% risk).
- **Pathophysiology:** ingestion of gluten peptides triggers a T-cell–mediated autoimmune response in genetically susceptible people (HLA-DQ2/DQ8). The result is inflammation and villous atrophy of the small intestine, leading to malabsorption of iron, calcium, folate, fat-soluble vitamins and other nutrients.
- **Treatment:** a lifelong strict gluten-free diet is the **only** established treatment. There is no medication that neutralises dietary gluten. Symptoms usually improve within weeks; mucosal healing can take 1–2 years and often requires detectable gluten removal.
- **Consequences of non-adherence:** persistent villous atrophy, micronutrient deficiencies (iron, B12, folate, vitamin D, calcium), osteoporosis, infertility, neuropathy, and an increased risk of enteropathy-associated T-cell lymphoma and other malignancies.

## Non-coeliac gluten sensitivity (NCGS)

- A clinical entity in which coeliac disease and wheat allergy have been excluded but symptoms (bloating, abdominal pain, fatigue, brain fog) improve on a GF diet and recur on blinded gluten challenge.
- Prevalence is uncertain; many people who self-identify as gluten-sensitive do not react to gluten under blinded conditions, and FODMAP carbohydrates (fructans in wheat) explain some symptoms.
- Management is a structured exclusion–rechallenge, ideally with a dietitian. The diet does not need to be as absolute as for coeliac disease, but meals should still be genuinely GF when the user selects this condition.

## Wheat allergy

- IgE-mediated reactions to wheat proteins (rapid onset, urticaria, angioedema, bronchospasm, anaphylaxis) or non-IgE reactions (wheat-dependent exercise-induced anaphylaxis, eosinophilic gastroenteritis).
- Different from coeliac disease: other gluten grains (barley, rye, oats) may be tolerated. Users with wheat allergy should also record it as an allergen.

## Cross-contact and the 20 ppm threshold

- The FDA limit of **<20 ppm** is based on analytical detection limits and evidence that most people with coeliac disease tolerate trace amounts at this level without histological harm.
- Cross-contact occurs in milling, transport, storage and food service. Shared toasters, fryers, pasta water, cutting boards and serving spoons are common sources.
- For meal generation, the practical rule is to **never add a gluten-containing ingredient**, even in small amounts, and to prefer naturally GF staples over GF-substitute processed foods.

## Oats

- Pure oats are GF, but conventional oats are contaminated with wheat/barley during growing, harvesting and processing.
- Only **certified gluten-free oats** are acceptable. Most people with coeliac disease tolerate certified GF oats in moderate amounts (typically ≤50–70 g/day), but a minority react to avenin.

## Hidden gluten

Common sources that meal planning must exclude:

- Soy sauce (wheat), teriyaki, hoisin, oyster sauce, some fish sauce blends.
- Bouillon cubes, stock powders, gravy granules, soup bases.
- Sausages, meatballs, burgers, deli meats, pâté (rusk, fillers).
- Imitation crab, surimi, breaded or battered fish.
- Malt vinegar, malt extract, brewer's yeast, beer, ale, lager.
- Flavoured crisps, salted snacks, some chocolate and sweets.
- Some sauces, dressings, marinades and spice blends.
- Communion wafers, some medications and supplements (excipients).

## Nutritional risks on a GF diet

- **Fibre:** GF breads and pasta are often refined and low in fibre. Whole GF grains (brown rice, quinoa, buckwheat, millet, teff, sorghum), legumes, vegetables and fruit should carry the fibre load.
- **Iron and folate:** fortification is less common in GF products; pair plant iron with vitamin C and include legumes and leafy greens.
- **Calcium and vitamin D:** important if dairy is limited or malabsorption has occurred; include calcium-set tofu, fortified plant milks, sardines with bones and leafy greens.
- **B vitamins:** whole GF grains, eggs, meat, fish and legumes.
- **Ultra-processed GF substitutes** are frequently higher in sugar, fat and additives; they should not become the base of the diet.

## Evidence grading

- Coeliac disease → strict GF diet: **Grade A** (standard of care, strong evidence).
- NCGS → GF diet: **Grade B** (consistent symptom improvement, heterogeneous mechanisms).
- Wheat allergy → wheat avoidance: **Grade A** for the allergy; not identical to a GF diet.
