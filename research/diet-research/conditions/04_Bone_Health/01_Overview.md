# Bone Health Diet — Overview

> Health-condition diet for people with osteoporosis, osteopenia, or elevated fracture risk. It supports bone mineral density and falls prevention through calcium, vitamin D, protein, vitamin K and magnesium — while limiting nutrients that increase bone loss.

---

## What "bone health" means nutritionally

Bone is living tissue that remodels continuously. Nutrition supports it through:

1. **Calcium** — the main mineral in bone. Food-first target: **1,000 mg/day** for most adults; **1,200 mg/day** for women over 50 and men over 70.
2. **Vitamin D** — enables calcium absorption and bone mineralisation. Target **800–1,000 IU/day** for adults at risk (600–800 IU is the general RDA; higher in deficiency, guided by blood levels).
3. **Protein** — bone matrix is ~50% protein by volume. Target **1.0–1.2 g/kg/day** for older adults, spread across meals.
4. **Vitamin K** — supports osteocalcin function; leafy greens are the main source.
5. **Magnesium** — structural component of bone; nuts, seeds, legumes and whole grains.
6. **Potassium and vitamin C** — support bone health indirectly through acid-base balance and collagen formation.

## Nutrients and habits that increase bone loss

- **Excess sodium** — increases urinary calcium excretion.
- **Excess alcohol** — impairs bone formation; >2 drinks/day is associated with lower bone density and higher fracture risk.
- **Excess caffeine** — >400 mg/day has a modest negative association with bone density, especially with low calcium intake.
- **High-dose retinol (preformed vitamin A)** — liver, cod liver oil and high-dose retinol supplements are associated with hip fracture risk.
- **Cola-type soft drinks** — associated with lower bone density (phosphoric acid, caffeine, displacement of calcium-rich drinks).
- **Very low-calorie diets and low protein intake** — accelerate bone and muscle loss.
- **Smoking** — independent risk factor for osteoporosis.

## Who it is for

| Condition | Notes |
|---|---|
| **Osteoporosis** | Nutrition supports medical treatment; it does not replace it |
| **Osteopenia** | Lifestyle nutrition can slow progression |
| **Postmenopausal women** | Accelerated bone loss after menopause; calcium and vitamin D are priorities |
| **Older adults** | Protein and vitamin D also support muscle mass and fall prevention |
| **Coeliac disease / malabsorption** | Treat the underlying cause; bone nutrition is especially important |
| **Long-term corticosteroid use** | High fracture risk; medical bone protection usually required |

## Core principles for meal generation

1. **Calcium at every meal** — dairy, fortified plant milk, calcium-set tofu, sardines/salmon with bones, low-oxalate leafy greens, almonds, tahini.
2. **Vitamin D sources** — oily fish, eggs, fortified foods; sunlight is the main source.
3. **Protein at every meal** — 25–35 g per main meal from fish, poultry, eggs, dairy, legumes or tofu.
4. **Vitamin K and magnesium** — leafy greens, broccoli, nuts, seeds, legumes, whole grains.
5. **Limit sodium** — no added salt, avoid processed and cured foods.
6. **Limit alcohol and cola drinks** — no more than moderate intake; ideally none.
7. **Avoid liver and cod liver oil** — very high preformed vitamin A.
8. **Include prunes** — 50 g/day has modest evidence for bone density preservation in postmenopausal women.

## What this diet is not

- Not a treatment for osteoporosis — medication, weight-bearing exercise and fall prevention are essential.
- Not a reason to take very high-dose calcium supplements; excess supplemental calcium (>1,500–2,000 mg/day) may increase kidney stone and cardiovascular risk.
- Not a guarantee against fractures.
