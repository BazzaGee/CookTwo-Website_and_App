# Bone Health Diet — Health Outcomes

## Bone mineral density

- Adequate calcium and vitamin D are required to achieve and maintain peak bone mass and to slow postmenopausal bone loss.
- Calcium plus vitamin D supplementation produces small but measurable increases in bone mineral density; effects are largest in people with low baseline intake or deficiency.
- Protein adequacy supports bone matrix and muscle mass; higher protein intake is associated with higher bone density in older adults when calcium intake is adequate.

## Fracture risk

- Calcium plus vitamin D supplementation reduces fracture risk modestly, particularly in institutionalised older adults with low intake.
- The largest fracture reductions come from combining nutrition with **weight-bearing and resistance exercise, fall prevention and osteoporosis medication** where indicated.
- Excess alcohol, smoking, low body weight and previous fractures are stronger risk factors than any single nutrient.

## Nutrient status

- Correcting vitamin D deficiency improves calcium absorption and reduces osteomalacia.
- Magnesium, vitamin K and potassium support bone quality but are not substitutes for calcium and vitamin D.
- High sodium intake increases urinary calcium loss; reducing sodium to ≤2,300 mg/day helps calcium balance.

## Risk reduction beyond bone

- The DASH and Mediterranean patterns are associated with lower fracture risk and better overall health, including cardiovascular benefits.
- Adequate protein and vitamin D also reduce sarcopenia and fall risk in older adults.
- Limiting alcohol reduces fall risk, which is a major contributor to fractures.

## Special populations

- **Postmenopausal women:** accelerated bone loss; calcium, vitamin D, protein and exercise are priorities.
- **Older men:** osteoporosis is underdiagnosed; same nutritional principles apply.
- **Coeliac disease and malabsorption:** bone loss is common; treat the underlying condition and monitor calcium/vitamin D.
- **Long-term corticosteroids:** high fracture risk; nutrition supports but does not replace bone-protective medication.
- **Chronic kidney disease:** calcium, phosphorus, potassium and vitamin D management must be individualised by nephrology.

## Caveats

- Calcium supplements are not risk-free: high doses may cause kidney stones and have been associated with cardiovascular calcification in some studies. Food-first is preferred.
- Very high vitamin D doses (>4,000 IU/day) can cause hypercalcaemia and are not recommended without medical supervision.
- Vitamin K interacts with warfarin; intake should be consistent and medically guided.
- This app provides meal guidance, not treatment for osteoporosis or fracture prevention.

## Evidence grading

- Calcium + vitamin D for bone density: **Grade A/B**.
- Protein adequacy for bone and muscle: **Grade B**.
- Sodium reduction for calcium balance: **Grade B**.
- Alcohol reduction for fracture risk: **Grade B**.
- Prunes for bone density: **Grade B** (small trials).
- Vitamin K for fracture prevention: **Grade B/C** (observational; mixed trials).
