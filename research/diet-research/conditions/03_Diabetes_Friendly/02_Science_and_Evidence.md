# Diabetes-Friendly Diet — Science & Evidence

## Medical nutrition therapy (MNT)

- MNT is a cornerstone of diabetes care and is recommended by the ADA for all people with diabetes and prediabetes.
- Randomised trials and systematic reviews show MNT lowers HbA1c by roughly **0.3–1.0%**, with the greatest effects when delivered by a registered dietitian and combined with medication and activity.
- There is **no single ideal macronutrient distribution**; the ADA emphasises food quality, energy balance and individualisation over a fixed ratio.

## Carbohydrate

- **Quality matters more than strict quantity.** Diets rich in non-starchy vegetables, whole fruits, legumes, whole grains, nuts and seeds are associated with better glycaemic control and lower cardiovascular risk.
- **Refined grains and added sugars** produce higher postprandial glucose and are consistently associated with worse outcomes.
- **Sugar-sweetened beverages** are the single strongest dietary driver of type 2 diabetes risk; eliminating them is a priority.
- **Carbohydrate consistency** across meals helps people on insulin or sulfonylureas match doses and reduce hypo/hyperglycaemia.
- **Very-low-carbohydrate diets** can improve HbA1c and triglycerides in the short term, but long-term adherence, LDL effects and hypoglycaemia risk require medical supervision.

## Glycaemic index and glycaemic load

- Low-GI/GL patterns produce modest HbA1c improvements (~0.2–0.5%) compared with high-GI patterns.
- The effect is smaller than overall diet quality and portion control, but low-GI choices are a useful practical tool.
- Low-GI staples: legumes, barley, oats, quinoa, whole-grain bread, sweet potato, most fruits, nuts and dairy.
- High-GI staples to limit: white bread, white rice, refined pasta, instant noodles, sugary cereals, sweets and sugary drinks.

## Fibre

- Higher fibre intake (25–30 g/day, or 14 g per 1,000 kcal) improves glycaemic control, lowers LDL cholesterol and increases satiety.
- Soluble fibre (oats, barley, legumes, psyllium) slows glucose absorption and modestly lowers HbA1c.
- Fibre-rich diets are associated with lower all-cause mortality in people with diabetes.

## Protein

- Protein has a low glycaemic impact and supports satiety and muscle mass.
- Plant proteins (legumes, tofu, nuts) are associated with lower cardiovascular risk.
- In people with diabetic kidney disease, protein intake should be individualised by a nephrologist or dietitian.

## Fat

- Replacing saturated fat with unsaturated fat lowers LDL cholesterol; Mediterranean-style patterns rich in olive oil, nuts and fish improve cardiovascular outcomes.
- The PREDIMED trial showed a ~30% reduction in major cardiovascular events with a Mediterranean diet supplemented with olive oil or nuts in high-risk participants, many with diabetes.

## Weight and remission

- In people with type 2 diabetes and overweight, **5–10% weight loss** improves HbA1c, blood pressure and lipids.
- Intensive weight-loss programmes (e.g. DiRECT) produced remission in a subset of participants, but remission is not guaranteed and requires long-term support.
- The app should support calorie targets without promoting crash dieting or disordered eating.

## Hypoglycaemia

- People on insulin or sulfonylureas are at risk of hypoglycaemia; skipping meals, very low carbohydrate intake and alcohol without food increase risk.
- The app must not suggest meal skipping, prolonged fasting, or insulin dose changes for these users.

## Evidence grading

- MNT for diabetes: **Grade A**.
- Fibre-rich, whole-food patterns: **Grade A/B**.
- Low-GI patterns: **Grade B**.
- Mediterranean pattern for cardiovascular risk: **Grade A**.
- Very-low-carbohydrate diets: **Grade B** short-term, **Grade C** long-term safety data.
- Weight loss for glycaemic improvement: **Grade A**.
