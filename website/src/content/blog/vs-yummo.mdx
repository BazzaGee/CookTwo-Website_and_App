# CookTwo vs Yummo: Two Approaches to Adaptive Cooking for Couples

You found an app that promises one meal, two plates â€” the same recipe, different portions for you and your partner. That is the starting point. Now you need to know whether it fits the rhythm of a shared kitchen, and whether it is a genuine yummo alternative or just a single-task tool that solves one slice of the problem.

![A shared kitchen where two people plate the same chicken and rice dish into two different portions, one smaller and one larger, both colourful and satisfying](/images/blog/vs-yummo-1.webp)

Yummo has gained attention as the first app built around the idea of adaptive portions for couples. CookTwo is built around the same idea. The difference is everything that happens before and after the recipe: how the pantry feeds the plan, how the grocery list syncs, and what actually changes when two people with two different goals share one kitchen.

## What Yummo does well

Yummo's core promise is compelling. Take a recipe, tell the app each partner's calorie and macro target, and the app splits the plating so both plates land in the right zone. For couples who cook the same meal but need different numbers, that is a genuine breakthrough.

The app also has a strong recipe library and a straightforward interface. If the primary need is portion scaling and the goal is something that works without much setup, it is a reasonable starting point. For couples whose only friction is portion size, it solves a real problem.

The app has been around long enough to build a loyal user base. Couples who just want to stop arguing over portions will find it does that job. For those who already enjoy cooking together and only need help with the numbers, it removes the friction of manual portion math and lets both partners see exactly how their plate maps to a target. It is not trying to be anything more than a recipe splitter with target-based plating, and there is something to be said for a tool that stays focused. The pricing model is straightforward, no tiered feature gating that pushes you toward upsells, which helps couples who do not want another subscription with separate accounts and confusing add-ons. As a yummo alternative for couples who value simplicity above all else, it holds its ground.

## Where Yummo stops

Where Yummo ends is where many couples real needs begin. The app treats the kitchen as a single event â€” choose a recipe, set two targets, get two plates. It does not address what happens on the other six days of the week. It has no pantry tracking. It has no real-time shared grocery list. It has no system for couples who want to cook together regularly rather than once in a while.

For couples managing different diets â€” one cutting, one bulk, one plant-based, one not â€” a recipe splitter is a partial fix. The full problem is planning a week of meals that respect both targets, shopping for both without duplicating ingredients, and cooking together without the mental load resting on one person. Yummo does not touch that.

The OnePoll statistic that couples argue 156 times a year about dinner is not hypothetical for couples relying on a tool that only handles today's meal. Without a system, the nightly stand-off continues â€” the app helps with the plating but not with the decision. That is the gap a proper yummo alternative needs to fill if it wants to serve couples beyond a single evening.

The mental load problem is particularly acute here. One partner almost always ends up being the default planner, the one who thinks through what is for dinner, who checks the fridge, who writes the shopping list. A tool that only scales portions does not distribute that work. It just makes the execution easier for the person who is already carrying the cognitive burden of the decision.

Research on household labour shows that women in heterosexual couples disproportionately handle meal planning, even when both partners work full time. An adaptive cooking tool that also shares the planning — not just the chopping — addresses a different category of friction than portion scaling alone.

## The practical difference in one week

A shared week of meals with Yummo looks like this: each partner picks a recipe, sets a profile, and the app plates two versions. The cooking is shared, but the planning is parallel. Two people, two lists, two sets of decisions every Sunday.

A shared week with CookTwo looks like this: the couple looks at the shared pantry, agrees on what to cook for the week, generates all five dinners in one pass, and the grocery list fills itself with what is missing. Sunday afternoon, two hours, five nights handled. Different macros on each plate. Same ingredients on both shopping trips. No duplicated products in the fridge.

The gap is not that Yummo cannot split a recipe. It is that CookTwo replaces the planning overhead entirely. That is what couples mean when they say they need a food system, not a recipe app. [Couples meal prep](/blog/couple-meal-prep) becomes less of a chore when the system handles the hardest part â€” the decisions. And [cooking one meal for two different macros](/blog/one-meal-two-macros) stops being a maths problem when the app does the splitting automatically. The key is that both partners hit their individual targets from the same cooking session.
