# CookTwo vs Eat This Much: The Individual Planner vs the Couples System

Eat This Much has built a reputation for one thing: generating meal plans that hit specific calorie and macro targets. Tell it your numbers, and it produces a daily menu that lands within your range. For one person following a plan, it works well. For two people who need to coordinate, it does not.

![A kitchen counter with two meal plans spread out — one for each partner — with different calorie targets and overlapping ingredients](/images/blog/vs-eat-this-much-1.webp)

CookTwo starts with the same goal — hitting different targets from shared ingredients — but builds the system around two people, not one. Where Eat This Much gives you a plan, CookTwo gives you a shared food system.

## What Eat This Much does well

Eat This Much's algorithm is genuinely strong at calorie targeting. Feed it your TDEE (Total Daily Energy Expenditure), your preferred diet style, and your macro ratios, and it generates a day's worth of meals that fit. The recipe database is large. The macro calculations are precise.

The [International Society of Sports Nutrition](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5852756/) recommends 1.4 to 2.0 g of protein per kilogram of body weight per day for active individuals, and Eat This Much's default targets generally align with that range. For individuals who want a hands-off meal plan with precise macro targeting, it is a solid tool.

The app also offers a wide range of diet filters — keto, paleo, vegetarian, vegan, Mediterranean, and more. You can exclude specific ingredients, set calorie ranges, and even specify meal timing. For a single person who wants granular control over their nutrition, the depth of customisation is impressive.

The recipe database is curated rather than scraped, which means the nutritional data is more reliable than apps that pull recipes from the open web. Each recipe includes a full macro breakdown and a link to the USDA FoodData Central entry for verification. For individuals who want precision, this level of detail is worth the subscription.

## Where Eat This Much stops

Eat This Much is built for one. There is no shared grocery list. There is no real-time sync. There is no way to merge two people's plans into a single shopping trip. There is no adaptive cooking for different targets.

For couples, this means either two separate plans — two shopping lists, two sets of ingredients, two cooking sessions — or one person's plan that the other person tries to adapt. Neither is sustainable.

The OnePoll survey that couples argue 156 times a year about dinner is not solved by a tool that generates two separate plans. The argument starts because the planning is not shared — and Eat This Much does not share it. One partner ends up doing all the thinking, all the planning, all the deciding.

The mental load problem is particularly acute here. Research on household labour shows that women in heterosexual couples disproportionately handle meal planning, even when both partners work full time. An alternative that also distributes the planning — not just the execution — addresses a different category of friction than individual nutrition tracking alone.

The financial cost is also worth considering. Two separate subscriptions for two separate plans means paying twice for the same service. The average couple using individual meal planning apps spends $100 per year on subscriptions that do not talk to each other. A shared system eliminates that redundancy.

The time cost is also significant. Each partner spends time setting up their own profile, entering their own preferences, and adjusting their own plan. When one partner changes a recipe, the other does not know. When one partner runs out of an ingredient, the other still plans to use it. The coordination overhead is invisible but real — it adds up to hours every week that a shared system eliminates.

## How CookTwo handles couples differently

CookTwo starts with the assumption that two people are making food decisions together. The plan is generated for both of you at once. The grocery list includes ingredients for both targets. The cooking is shared. The plating diverges.

The [one-meal-two-plates](/one-meal-two-plates) method is the core: one recipe, two plates, different macros. The [macro maths](/blog/one-meal-two-macros) are handled automatically. The shared grocery list syncs in under 100 milliseconds. The pantry tracks what you already have so you are not buying duplicates.

## The practical difference in one week

A shared week of meals with Eat This Much looks like this: each partner generates their own daily plan, shops from their own list, and cooks from their own recipes. The cooking is parallel, not shared. Two people, two kitchens, two sets of ingredients.

A shared week with CookTwo looks like this: the couple looks at the shared pantry, agrees on what to cook for the week, generates all five dinners in one pass, and the grocery list fills itself with what is missing. Sunday afternoon, two hours, five nights handled. Different macros on each plate. Same ingredients on both shopping trips. No duplicated products in the fridge.

The gap is not that Eat This Much cannot plan meals. It is that it plans for one person at a time. That is the difference a proper eat this much alternative needs to bridge if it wants to serve couples.

## Which one should you choose?

If you are cooking for yourself and want precise macro targeting with minimal setup, Eat This Much is a strong individual tool. It does one job well.

If you are cooking for two and need both partners to hit different targets from shared ingredients, CookTwo is built for that. It is not an eat this much alternative that adds couples features to an individual tool. It is a different category — a shared food system designed for two people in one kitchen.

The key difference is that CookTwo was designed for the shared kitchen from day one. The mental load distributes naturally because the system is built around two people, not two individual accounts bolted together. That is the difference that matters for couples.

The practical test is simple: can you cook one recipe and plate it two ways? Eat This Much cannot — it generates two separate plans. CookTwo can — one recipe, two plates, different macros. The difference is not just features. It is architecture. CookTwo was designed for two people in one kitchen from the start.
