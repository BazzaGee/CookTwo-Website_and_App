High-protein is not a diet philosophy. It is a single lever — protein at roughly 30% of calories, typically 1.6–2.2 g/kg body weight — that slots into Mediterranean, low-carb, plant-based, fasting-course, or omnivorous plans without asking anyone to bankrupt a food category. That versatility is also why the data runs so coherently: raising protein has primarily *within-diet* effects on appetite, lean mass and maintenance, and does not ask you to overturn your entire nutrition system to reach the results. On this map it is the most compatible tool; a tool to solve once and reuse in every plan: satiety, lean mass, and retention do the heavy lifting.

Also: given the word momentum, most "high-protein" labels — processed snack bars, protein-laden desserts, fast food with a "protein" line — do not carry the pattern's actual benefits. What is actually doing the work of this pattern is food: eggs, fish, poultry, lean meat, dairy, legumes and soy in measured amount.

## What the evidence actually shows

<StudyCard name="Weigle et al. — the spontaneous appetite drop (American Journal of Clinical Nutrition, 2005)" design="Controlled feeding phases" participants="19 healthy adults" duration="~12 weeks" finding="Moving protein from 15% to 30% of calories at fixed fiber and fat brought spontaneous ~441 kcal/day drops in intake, weight loss and reduced hunger — with no calorie counting, no instructions, no food choices. Protein is the most reliable appetite lever." url="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16002798/" />

<StudyCard name="Wycherley et al. — deficit trials on high-protein versus standard (American Journal of Clinical Nutrition, 2012)" design="Meta-analysis of RCTs" participants="—" duration="—" finding="High-protein diets produced an extra −0.79 kg weight and −0.87 kg fat versus standard-protein, with better triglyceride changes and — importantly — better lean-mass retention across deficit conditions." url="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23097268/" />

<StudyCard name="Morton et al. — resistance training and protein (British Journal of Sports Medicine, 2018)" design="Systematic review and meta-analysis" participants="Dozens of trials on protein plus resistance training" duration="—" finding="Muscle-gain returns to protein scale until roughly 1.62 g/kg/day is reached — a smart threshold for lifters; below that, protein demands are proportionate and not escalational." url="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29179536/" note="The gym-bro 2 g/kg figure is largely marketing; 1.6 g/kg is where the meta-analysis centre of mass sits." />

<StudyCard name="Shams-White et al. — protein and bone health (American Journal of Clinical Nutrition, 2017)" design="Systematic review / meta-analysis" participants="—" duration="—" finding="Higher protein supported modest gains in bone mineral density — reversing the protein-leaches-calcium folklore — with reduced hip fracture risk. Useful when calcium, vitamin D and vegetables are in place." url="https://doi.org/10.3945/ajcn.116.145180" />

Protein's final leg is the one it is most falsified on — kidney function. In the 2018 systematic review from Devries et al., healthy adults on higher-protein intakes compared to normal- or lower-protein cohorts showed no difference in measured kidney function — including across 3.5 g/kg body weight entries. GFR rose slightly, which is an *adaptive hyperfiltration*, not damage, so long as the healthy kidney is starting intact.

## How to build it

<Callout variant="tip" title="Protein anchors: the practical list">
Eggs (6 g each), Greek yogurt, cottage cheese and skyr; chicken, turkey, lean beef, pork tenderloin; oily fish and tinned fish; tofu, tempeh, lentils, chickpeas, black beans; whey/pea protein as a supplement when targets miss. Protein-per-plate anchors at 25–35 g per meal and a 0.2–0.4 g/kg pre-sleep is where the adult-day principles land.
</Callout>
