Most people, most of the time, do not want a named diet. They want to eat well without a thesis: real food for dinner, the nice dessert, the takeaway night, one shared grocery run. Nutrition science has a name for this *absence of a name* — it is the general healthy-eating pattern that every major health authority on earth converged on, and on this map it grades **A**: the *least* restrictive option with the *most* agreed evidence behind it.

What makes it interesting is why it wins. Not because balance is a virtue in the abstract, but because in the head-to-head trials — DIETFITS, the largest umbrella review to date — flexible whole-food patterns equalled the restrictive ones on weight and beat them on markers, and the largest observational grades show the healthiest populations clustered in the moderate-middle of the macro distribution rather than at any extreme.

## What "healthy eating" means to the guidelines

Broad ranges rather than narrow targets — and the fact that each authority independently chose wide bands is itself informative: **this is what a decade of clear evidence looks like.** In nutritional language (world bodies, 2018–2024 convergence):

- **Carbohydrate**: 45–65% of energy — whole grains, fruit, legumes, vegetables, not refined starch
- **Protein**: ~10–35% of energy (the WHO recommends at minimum 0.83 g/kg/day for adults)
- **Fat**: 20–35% of energy, with saturated fat under 10%
- **Sugar**: free sugars under 10% of energy (ideally 5%)
- **Produce**: 400+ grams of fruit and vegetables daily
- **Sodium**: below 5 g/day salt, generally

No single thing is forbidden — organised *as* a diet, the plate looks like the flexible version we've already written up across [volumetrics](/diets/volumetrics), [Mediterranean](/diets/mediterranean) or [DASH](/diets/dash). What makes it "no diet" is the *ownership shift*: no brand, no phase, no counting machinery, no identity to defend.

## The two failure modes to know

**Drift.** Without a named plan, the pattern's *scaffolding* disappears — default sugary drinks creep back, portions creep, and home cooking gives way. The fix is a couple of structural habits rather than rules: cook dinner, make the shared base a starch-and-vegetable structure, shop from a list (the [pantry-first](/pantry-first-cooking) approach in CookTwo is designed exactly for this), keep fruit visible, keep any sweetened drink out of the weekly default.

**Optimisation anxiety.** The other failure is turning "healthy" into a weight-loss *chase*, at which point you simply need one of the goal-directed patterns on this map.

## Whom this pattern suits

- **The majority of adults** — genuinely now, in every health-body recommendation ever attempted.
- **Couples with different dietary goals**: one shared healthy base with per-person portions solves the two-plate problem (that is literally the CookTwo app's design principle).
- **Anyone for whom the named patterns feel unsustainable** — this pattern is the first-floor apartment of healthy eating: less height, more flexible furniture.
