Paleo carries a name that makes scientific claims it then has to live up to. The story — that our current epidemic is a mismatch with an eating environment our hominin evolution fitted — has some truth in it. The stronger version of the argument, the one that dominates marketing, treats the Paleolithic as a nutrition Garden of Eden and grains, dairy and legumes as invaders of an otherwise optimal template. The second claim is literary, not scientific, and the literature is clear-eyed enough to know it.

Here is the irony to hold throughout: the trials exist, and they show real benefits — bigger glucose tolerance improvements than Mediterranean in one head-to-head, better waist and blood-pressure outcomes in a meta-analysis, a mortality-linked Swedish cohort favouring the pattern *built on fish and plants* rather than steak. Where paleo wins, it almost always wins for the boring reason: eating real food instead of an ultra-processed diet. Where it loses is the exclusion logic — because the exclusions include foods, especially legumes and dairy, that carry established health benefit.

## The trials, in order of honesty

<StudyCard name="Lindeberg et al. — paleo vs Mediterranean for glucose tolerance (Diabetologia, 2007)" design="Randomized controlled trial" participants="29 adults with ischaemic heart disease" duration="12 weeks" finding="Paleo drove bigger glucose-tolerance improvement than a Mediterranean-like diet — including greater insulin AUC reduction. The first head-to-head with a gold-standard comparison diet for paleo-grade eating." url="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17583796/" note="n=29, 12-week duration; legitimate finding, small trial." />

<StudyCard name="Frassetto et al. — 10 days of paleo in healthy volunteers (European Journal of Clinical Nutrition, 2009)" design="Controlled feeding intervention" participants="9 non-obese sedentary adults" duration="10 days" finding="Blood pressure, lipid profile, insulin sensitivity and glucose handling improved despite no weight change — a very short window, but one of the few pieces of evidence improvements can precede weight loss." url="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19209185/" />

<StudyCard name="Manheimer et al. — the paleo meta-analysis (American Journal of Clinical Nutrition, 2015)" design="Systematic review and meta-analysis" participants="159 adults across 4 RCTs" duration="2 weeks to 2 years" finding="Paleo beat the reference diets it was compared to, across waist (−3.5 cm), triglycerides, systolic BP (−3.6 mmHg), HDL and fasting glucose — with the authors explicitly requiring longer and larger trials before recommending." url="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26269362/" note="The most-cited papers broadly support the short-term metabolic claims and unchanged the long-term question." />

<StudyCard name="Whalen et al. — paleo score and mortality over 20 years (Journal of Nutrition, 2017)" design="Prospective cohort (Swedish)" participants="21,423 adults" duration="Up to 20 years" finding="Higher paleo-score adherence tracked with lower all-cause (HR 0.74), cardiovascular and cancer mortality — with the protective version the one anchored on fish, fruit and vegetables instead of red meat. The longevity signal went to the plant-heavy paleo." url="https://doi.org/10.1093/jn/nwx006" note="Observational; paleo-adjacent score, not uniform paleo adherence." />

Two threads of moderate circulating evidence add spread. The autoimmune-protocol spin-off (AIP) — a strict elimination version — shows small pilot results for inflammatory conditions including IBD in small trials. On the other side, the Seidelmann/Lancet Public Health dose-response data firmly favour a moderate carbohydrate middle, with noticeably adverse mortality signal on the animal-heavy end; paleo's usual macro layout — moderate-to-low carb, 30% protein — sits within the middle, never at the edges.

## The exclusions case: where paleo claims outpace evidence

**Legumes.** No robust evidence supports avoiding them. Cohorts associate legumes with reduced cardiovascular and cancer risk, better glycaemic control, and — strongly — higher fibre intake. The lectin worry is an internet-era concern that no realistic cooking dose has ever shown to matter.

**Whole grains.** The exclusion faces extra headwind: whole grains remain in most cardiovascular protective statements, weight as energy density, and the global patterns show populations over 60% carb sit across health extremes — refined vs whole being the variable that matters.

**Dairy.** Complex and population-specific; fermented dairy generally tracks neutrally or positively in mortality cohorts. The calcium drop is real and young women and adolescents need the most attention.

**What paleo *gets right* with full force: refined grains, sugar, industrial vegetable oil and ultra-processed products.** These get substantial support in the evidence base, and no long-lived or trial-tested pattern disagrees.

Read paleo as a strict whole-food enforcer rather than a history lesson. Strip the ancestral mythology and run its strongest versions: lean and oily fish, vegetables at every meal, fruit in season, nuts, no industry product — and treat grains, dairy and legumes as borderline negotiable rather than toxic.
</Callout>

## The catches worth naming

- **Calcium.** Dairy-free paleo makes the 1,000+ mg/day calcium target genuinely difficult if not supplemented; canned fish with bones, leafy greens and almonds only carry so far.
- **Whole-grain and legume fibre.** Fibre-specific benefits the meta-analyses lean on — cholesterol lowering, microbiome diversity, SCFA production from butyrate — mostly live in foods paleo drops.
- **Cost.** Grass-fed emphasis and nut consumption push real grocery bills; canned fish and frozen vegetables flatten the gap as always.
- **Socially.** Restaurants are workable; dinner parties mostly require fortification of purpose — precisely why the exclusive version isn't hospitalised long-term.
- **Where the "ancestral" framing actually lands.** The Paleolithic hominin picture is genuinely contested — a diverse, geographically varied eating landscape that paleo usually flattens. Quickly speaking: no ancient population on this hub's global map (Arctic traditional diets included) had modern supermarket access, and none relied on modern reduced-fiber legume or grain culture.

## Who it suits

- **People who respond well to bright-line rules** — paleo draws cleaner prohibitions than Mediterranean. If a rule keeps you honest, that is a liveability point in a diet science context.
- **Quick whole-food resets and blood-sugar work**, where the restrictions, unlike mainstream alternatives, avoid refined starch staples.
- Not for: pregnancy, teenage athletes, or anyone with a house allergy to rules.
- Not binary: a "paleo-shaped week" — ancestral carbs in, modern processing out, moderate grain and dairy room — yields nearly the same result without nearly the same accountability.
