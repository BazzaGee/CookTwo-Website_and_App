The carnivore diet sits in a category of one on this map: it is the only pattern for which the *entire* evidence base consists of historical anecdotes, one pro-carnivore survey, and a handful of clinic case reports from a single Hungarian practice. There are no randomized trials. There are no cohorts. There are no meta-analyses. No major health organization endorses it for any condition, for any length of time, for any population.

And yet it deserves a real guide, not a dismissal — because thousands of people are about to try it this month, some because it fixed real problems in ways standard advice did not. This page does three things. It states plainly what the evidence actually contains. It explains the mechanism stories that are plausible versus the ones that are speculation. And it lays out the genuine risks — including the ones that a checklist of blood tests could catch in time.

## What the evidence actually contains

## The mechanisms that are real, and the ones that are hope

**What plausibly helps.** Protein at high levels drives satiety, suppresses appetite and spares muscle — all documented. Ultra-processed food, refined sugar and refined starch vanish permanently — the single most-evidence-backed dietary change in nutrition. Ketosis arrives via zero carb. And when someone feels dramatically better on carnivore, the honest explanations are real: they removed alcohol, seed oil, hyperpalatable energy-dense food; they upped protein and protein-satiety; in some cases they unknowingly removed FODMAPs or trigger foods for a digestive condition.

**What remains speculation.** The claim that plants are inherently harmful, that fibre is unnecessary, that "plants defend themselves with toxins" justifies total avoidance, or that saturated fat stops mattering when carbohydrate is zero. Purely mechanistic argument in all four cases, with cohort data consistently pointing the other way — the animal-based low-carb arm of the 2018 Lancet Public Health meta-analysis carried *higher* mortality than plant-constructed versions.

**The vitamin C question.** Stefansson and Anderson avoided scurvy, and modern carnivore anecdotes frequently go a decade-plus without it. The proposed mechanism — glucose competes with vitamin C at the GLUT receptor, so low glucose spares vitamin C — is plausible and unproven, and long-term vitamin C status has never been formally studied on carnivore. Fresh liver and some fish roe contain small amounts. Nobody should assume the year-long n=2 result applies to them.

## The risks you should not get talked down about

**LDL.** Zero-carb, high-saturated-fat eating reproduces the same LDL jump we describe for keto — potentially larger, because plants (and their polyphenols and monounsaturated fat) are gone. In a tracking-lipids subset on keto this happens for ~25–30%; carnivore's subset data is unknown by sampling design. Baseline lipid panel including ApoB before starting, repeat at three months, is the non-negotiable minimum.

**Fibre and the microbiome.** Zero fibre, ever. Short-term side effects are widely reported — constipation or sludge in the other direction. Long-term, cohort work consistently shows plant-rich patterns associate with healthier microbiomes and lower mortality; nothing on the carnivore side rebuts this because nothing has measured it.

**Kidney.** Very high protein and very low citrate with concentrated urine is the textbook milieu for stones; the paediatric epilepsy keto literature shows a 3–7% stone rate even with vegetables. Hydration and regular urinalysis are cheap insurance.

**Who should never attempt it.** Type 1 diabetes without an endocrinologist; anyone on SGLT2 inhibitors; hypothyroid follow through unsupervised; anyone with kidney disease or a personal or family history of severe LDL elevation; pregnant and breastfeeding women; children and teenagers; anyone with disordered-eating history — this pattern takes food-rule rigidity to its logical endpoint.

At risk of burying the lede: **this pattern is run responsibly, if at all, with a stack of blood work** — lipids with ApoB at baseline and three months, kidney function, urinalysis for stones, and an honest annual review with a clinician who knows what is in it.
