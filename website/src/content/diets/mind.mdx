Most diet claims about the brain are folklore in a lab coat. MIND is the exception with a research paper trail — a hybrid of the two most rigorously tested patterns in nutrition, Mediterranean and DASH, rescored specifically for foods that show up again and again in cognition cohorts. Rush University's nutrition epidemiologists built the 15-point MIND score in the 2010s, applied it to their Chicago ageing cohorts, and found something unusual even by the standards of diet-brain research: a 53% lower rate of incident Alzheimer's disease among the strictest adherents, and — this is the quietly better part — a 35% reduction at only *moderate* adherence.

The catch, stated up front because this hub does not do bait-and-switch: all of that is observational. There is no completed randomized trial — the U.S. POINTER study, funded by NIH, is running now. So the honest framing is "robust association", not "proven cure", and this guide holds that line throughout.

## What MIND is

Ten foods to lean on, five to shrink — 15 rules total, weighted into a score:

**Encouraged:** green leafy vegetables (daily), other vegetables (daily), nuts (most days), berries (twice a week), beans (most days), whole grains (three daily servings), fish (weekly), poultry (twice weekly), olive oil as the default fat, and a glass of wine (optional).

**Limited:** red meat (under four servings weekly), butter and stick margarine (under a tablespoon daily), cheese (under a serving weekly), pastries and sweets (under five weekly), and fried or fast food (under once weekly).

What makes MIND distinctive is the priority list, not the philosophy. Leafy greens get their own daily line — the single food category most strongly tied to slower cognitive decline in the Rush cohorts. Berries — blueberries, strawberries — are the fruit group with the best cognitive data, promoted over "eat fruit, generically". And unlike its parents, MIND allows only limited cheese rather than DASH-style fixed low-fat servings.

## The evidence, and what it can honestly claim

<StudyCard name="Morris et al. — slower cognitive decline (Alzheimer's & Dementia, 2015)" design="Prospective cohort" participants="960 older adults" duration="~4.7 years" finding="High MIND adherence was associated with significantly slower global cognitive decline, equivalent to being cognitively younger by a meaningful margin." url="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22683954/" />

<StudyCard name="Leafy greens alone (Neurology, 2018)" design="Prospective cohort" participants="960 elders" duration="~10 years" finding="The group eating roughly one serving of green leafy vegetables per day had cognitive decline matching people about 11 years younger — the dataset behind MIND's daily-greens rule." url="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29263222/" />

Beyond the Rush cohorts, the 2019 systematic review pooled Mediterranean, DASH and MIND studies and reported a consistent 35–40% reduction in Alzheimer's risk for high adherence, with MIND out-associating its parents in head-to-head model comparisons. The mechanistic story is plausible and multi-route: berry anthocyanins cross the blood-brain barrier; olive-oil oleocanthal speeds amyloid-beta clearance in lab work; omega-3 DHA is structural neuronal membrane; and the vascular thread — blood-pressure control carrying oxygen to brain tissue — runs straight back to DASH. That last one may matter most, because what shows up as "Alzheimer's and dementia" in the neighbourhood is frequently mixed vascular dementia.

<Callout variant="warning" title="What the evidence is not">
Everything above comes from food-frequency questionnaires in largely white urban Chicago cohorts, and observational design cannot rule out residual confounding — people who eat this way also tend to smoke less, walk more and read more labels. The Rush team addressed reverse causation (early dementia changing diet) by excluding the first two years of follow-up and the association held. But "association, replicated, no completed RCT" is precisely where the claim honestly sits until POINTER reads out.
</Callout>

## Doing MIND in a real kitchen

- **One greens purchase per shop.** Spinach/kale bags, or frozen, whichever gets eaten. The 11-years-younger effect came from one serving a day.
- **Frozen berries in the freezer door.** MIND's berry data comes from blueberries and strawberries; frozen counts fully.
- **Nuts within arm's reach.** A palmful daily trims inflammation, vitamin E and the snacking-machine problem.
- **Olive oil replaces butter at every decision point.** This single swap moves the MIND score more than people expect.
- **Fish once, poultry twice, legumes most days.** Protein stays adequate without a food-scale operation.
- **Cheese becomes a condiment, not a course.** The weekly cap is generous enough for a weekend pizza night.

## Who MIND suits

- **Anyone whose family history makes dementia the risk they want to attack** — combined with exercise and BP control, this is the strongest diet-flavoured lever documented.
- **Mediterranean-following households** — MIND is a rescored cousin, and a hybrid week is trivial.
- **Older adults wanting a durable pattern** — the food is soft, cheap and familiar.
- Not for anyone expecting a 30-day outcome. Brain health compounds on decade horizons, like pension contributions.
