The gluten-free diet wears two faces with utterly different evidence grades. On one: **the strict gluten-free diet is a grade-A medical necessity** for coeliac disease — an autoimmune condition where a gluten protein peptide triggers T-cell attacks on intestinal villi and leads to malabsorption, iron/folate/calcium deficiencies, osteoporosis, infertility and, at the extreme, malignant transformation. Its only treatment is strict lifetime gluten exclusion; there is no pill that neutralises gluten. On the other face: the gluten-free diet as a *general health upgrade* is unsupported — and the honest data is more complicated: gluten-free *processed* substitutes tend to carry **less** fibre, less iron, less folate and less B vitamin fortification and more added sugar or fat than their wheat-based equivalents.

This page encompasses both stories as honestly as our evidence base allows, and answers the kitchen questions — cross-contact, the 20-ppm rule, testing before restriction — that a coeliac diagnosis makes life-urgent.

## What the science is settled on

<Callout variant="quote" title="The first rule of the gluten-free diet">
**Test before restrict.** The screening tests for coeliac disease — tTG-IgA antibodies and biopsy evidence — need gluten *on board* in the diet to be positive. Going gluten-free before testing hides the disease without treating it, and may cause years of diagnostic confusion. Talk to a GP or gastroenterologist before you self-restrict.
</Callout>

Three variants demand the gluten-free plate with different grades of strictness:

1. **Coeliac disease.** Grade A. Approximate 1% population prevalence; requires strict lifetime exclusion — a clinical diagnosis. Villous atrophy heals over 1–2 years once the diet is genuinely strict; hidden traces keep inflammation simmering.
2. **Non-coeliac gluten sensitivity (NCGS).** Grade B. A real symptom-improvement zone for those where coeliac and wheat allergy are excluded, with symptoms returning with blinded gluten challenge. Prevalence remains uncertain: many people self-identify as gluten-sensitive and do not react under blinded challenge — with some causes pointing to fructans instead (see the [low FODMAP guide](/diets/low-fodmap)). Management: structured exclusion-rechallenge with a dietitian.
3. **Wheat allergy.** Separate, IgE-mediated and *not* identical with gluten-free — many wheat-allergic people tolerate barley, rye and oats; others have anaphylactic activation. Recorded as an allergy on the app, not as a diet.

## Cross-contact is the clinical half of the diet

The regulatory **under 20 ppm** ceiling is what "gluten-free" legally means — a threshold set by detection capability and by evidence that most coeliac patients tolerate traces at that level. What the legal limit quietly implies is hard: shared fryers, pasta water, cutting boards, toasters, serving spoons, bulk bins and baked-good manufacturing lines are all plausible cross-contact surfaces. The practical household answer is separation: a dedicated toaster, labelled spreads, and distinct pots and boards for the strict-GF member.

## Building the nutrition on purpose

The classic GF failure mode is shop-substitution: swap gluten bread for GF bread, gluten pasta for GF pasta, and call it nutrition — where each swap is normally **lower in** fibre, iron, folate, sun fortification and **higher in** sugar and refined starch. The well-planned GF week instead stays with naturally GF options: rice (brown or wild), quinoa, buckwheat, millet, sorghum, GF oats (labelled), legumes, potatoes and fortified milk staples. These are the same food-quality shifts that this hub emphasises throughout — with the mandatory strictness of disease management that *nobody* should take up without reason.
