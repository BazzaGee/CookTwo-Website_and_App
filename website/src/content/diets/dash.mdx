DASH is what happens when nutrition is treated like a pharmaceutical. It was never a trend or a philosophy — it was an instrument, commissioned by the NIH and NIH-funded trials were run to measure it. A dietary pattern was engineered, then a placebo-controlled-equivalent feeding trial was conducted in controlled kitchens, and the endpoint — millimetres of mercury — was measured with the same sphygmomanometers used in drug trials. The result: in people with hypertension, the DASH diet drops systolic pressure by up to 11.4 mmHg, in the same single-digit magnitude physicians expect from one blood-pressure medication, without a prescription pad.

This guide covers precisely what the trials showed, what is inside the pattern, and the practical catches — including the fact that the salt shaker in your kitchen is nearly irrelevant.

## What DASH is

The name is the mission statement: **Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension**. The pattern presses five levers in fixed servings rather than grams-and-philosophy:

- 4–5 servings of **vegetables** and 4–5 of **fruit** daily
- 6–8 servings of **whole grains**
- 2–3 servings of **low-fat dairy**
- Up to 6 daily ounces of **lean meat, poultry or fish** — about two palm-sized servings
- 4–5 weekly servings of **nuts, seeds and legumes**
- Fats, sweets and red meat capped; sodium held around 2,300 mg/day, or 1,500 mg/day for people who need aggressive BP control

Potassium, calcium and magnesium are the unsung benefits: DASH raises potassium to roughly the 4,700 mg/day that blood-pressure physiology rewards, chiefly from produce. The macro split lands at about 55% carb, 18% protein, 27% fat — the moderate middle of the map, which this hub argues the entire evidence base favours.

## The trials that built it

<StudyCard name="DASH-Sodium (NEJM, 2001)" design="Randomized crossover feeding trial" participants="412 adults" duration="30 days per condition" finding="DASH at every sodium level beat a typical American diet. <StudyCard name="OMNIHeart (JAMA, 2005)" design="Randomized 3-period crossover" participants="164 adults" duration="6 weeks per arm" finding="Swapping 10% of carbohydrate for protein or for unsaturated fat improved blood pressure and lipids beyond standard DASH — evidence that the pattern has adjustable wings rather than one true shape." url="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16287956/" />

<StudyCard name="ENCORE (Arch Intern Med, 2010)" design="RCT" participants="144 overweight adults with elevated BP" duration="16 weeks" finding="DASH plus exercise and structured weight loss dropped roughly 8.7 kg and further improved blood pressure — an additive, not competing, effect." url="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20101007/" />

Down the outcomes chain, the long cohorts add the population-scale picture. In Nurses' Health Study follow-ups past 100,000 participants, highest DASH adherence tracked with ~14% lower coronary heart disease and ~17% lower stroke; the 2013 meta-analysis of DASH-style cohorts recorded ~20% fewer cardiovascular events overall. DASH also improved fasting glucose and insulin resistance markers, reduced LDL cholesterol (around 11 mg/dL) in the maintenance trials, and quietly produced the MIND diet, which we review [separate guide](/diets/mind).

## The lever nobody knows they are pulling

The daily sodium target in DASH-Sodium is 2,300 mg — under a teaspoon of salt. Most adults take in near 3,400 mg/day, and about three-quarters of it rides on processed food, restaurant meals and bread, not on your stove. That is the fibre of the DASH gap: cooking real food at home already does the sodium work. If DASH feels hard in a takeaway culture, that is exactly why — the diet is not asking for culinary heroics, it is asking you to bypass the industrial salt pipeline.

<Callout variant="tip" title="The 10-food swap that covers most of it">
Canned beans rinsed (rinsing removes ~40% of sodium), no-salt-added canned tomatoes, unsalted nuts, plain frozen vegetables, low-sodium broth, whole-grain bread checked at the label — 6 choices, plus seasoning food with acid (lemon, vinegar), heat (chilli), herbs (basil, coriander, thyme) instead of salt covers the rest. In blood-pressure terms, this is the healthy-parts version of a salt-free diet that neither tastes nor feels like one.
</Callout>

## Risks and honest limitations

**Kidney caution.** DASH leans on potassium — great for clear corporate arterial health, less so in advanced chronic kidney disease, where potassium handling is impaired and protein targets differ. If CKD is in the picture, the diet gets modified with a nephrologist and renal dietitian — the same people you were already seeing.

**Medication interactions.** If you are on antihypertensives, this diet is meant to be combined with your pharmacology, not to replace it overnight. Blood pressure can drop propranolol-drug style when potassium rises and sodium falls; measure, and let your prescriber adjust.

**Fruit cost and access.** DASH assumes produce access. Frozen vegetables carry identical potassium and cost a third; canned fruit in juice, not syrup, fills the rest.

**Modest HDL dip.** In the original trial, HDL fell slightly and triglycerides barely moved. The cardiovascular outcome data still won — total cholesterol and LDL fell — and OMNIHeart variants show the trade can be tuned.

## Who DASH is for

- **Anyone with raised blood pressure — full stop.** This is the evidence-first standard-of-care diet it was designed for.
- **People trying to prevent the first stroke**, where blood pressure is the strongest modifiable lever.
- **Couples** — the servings fit naturally into shared cooking; the sodium work happens at the pantry level, not at each plate.
- **Anyone who wants a heart-healthy version with the numbers published.** Where Mediterranean is the tested culture, DASH is the tested instrument. Both grade A; choose by taste.
