"Plant-based" is the umbrella term for eating patterns that make plants the structural backbone of the plate — with the animal kingdom filtering through at different doses. Vegan removes all of it. Vegetarian keeps eggs and dairy. Pescatarian keeps fish. Flexitarian keeps most options while skewing weekly. Each is on this map as its own guide; this page is the summary document and covers the pattern family, then links to each for the exact nutrients.

What makes the pattern worth its own guide is that the gradient across these sub-patterns shares findings: people eating more plants — almost irrespective of what remains — track lower body weight, lower blood pressure, lower diabetes prevalence and improved cholesterol. The dose-response in the Adventist data has a clean order: vegan under vegetarian under pescatarian under omnivore. That pure monotonicity is what makes the pattern genuinely good science rather than one more ideology.

## The evidence structure

<StudyCard name="Orlich et al. — the Adventist dose-response (JAMA Internal Medicine, 2013)" design="Prospective cohort, five dietary patterns" participants="73,308 Adventists" duration="~5.8 years" finding="All-cause mortality fell stepwise as animal intake fell: lacto-ovo vegetarians HR 0.91, vegans HR 0.85, pescatarians HR 0.81 — with cardiometabolic mortality significantly lower across all vegetarian patterns. <StudyCard name="Yokoyama et al. — vegetarian diets and blood pressure (JAMA Internal Medicine, 2014)" design="Meta-analysis (7 RCTs + 32 cohorts)" participants="21,604 in cohorts, 311 in trials" duration="—" finding="Vegetarian patterns dropped blood pressure 4.8/2.2 mmHg in trials and 6.9/4.7 mmHg in cohorts — an effect size comparable to single-drug therapy, and one of the most replicated findings in plant-based nutrition." url="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24566949/" />

<Callout variant="quote" title="The cleanest dose-response in cohort science">
Between the pattern grades in the Adventist cohorts, as plant intake dominates the plate and animal intake recedes, every metabolic measure moves one direction: weight down, blood pressure down, cholesterol down, diabetes risk down. What does not exist anywhere in this dataset is an "ultra-processed vegan" subgroup with health outcomes to match the vegans eating lentils — it is the plants, in whole form, suppressing, not the animal category being ideological.
</Callout>

## The two honest failures of plant-based eating

**The animal-losing, nutrient-losing diets.** The diet performs well only if each meal earns protein somewhere: legumes, soy, tofu, tempeh, nuts, or fish where the sub-pattern permits. For every studied reanalysis, it is the whole-food legume-and-nut-heavy pattern campuses benefit — always the fallback is grain-plus-vegetable-of-necessity, never packaged patties.

**Vitamin B12.** Non-negotiable. On a purely plant-based diet with no supplementation, B12 status collapses over 1–2 years — the nutrient is in animal biology only. This is precisely what guidance latest has been for decades, and the data not needing further forwards.

<Callout variant="tip" title="The two-directional B12 rule">
B12 supplementation is one of the rare nutrition places where the fix is simple: the supplement form is inexpensive and effective. Every vegan-supportive nutrition body recommends an ongoing B12 source; pescatarians and semi-vegetarians get substantial amounts from fish or dairy and only rarely need one. There is no vegan food law that repairs this gap — fortified foods (nutritional yeast, plant milk) lean on the same nutrient.
</Callout>

## The plant-based nutrient checklist

| Nutrient | Why it requires planning | Where it comes from |
|---|---|---|
| Vitamin B12 | No plant supplies it reliably | Supplement, fortified plant milk, nutritional yeast |
| Iron | Lower bioavailability in plant sources | Lentils, tofu, pumpkin seeds with vitamin C | 
| Calcium | Dairy absent in strict versions | Calcium-set tofu, fortified milks, kale, almonds |
| Omega-3 (EPA/DHA) | ALA conversion is inefficient | Fish (pescatarian), walnuts, flax, chia; algae oil for strict vegans |
| Vitamin D | Limited in diets of all patterns | Sunlight, fortified plant milk, supplements |
| Iodine | Variable alongside seaweed | Iodized salt, occasional seaweed |
| Zinc | Lower in grain- and legume-based diets | Legumes, cashews, pumpkin seeds |

The required attention is real — and also worth stating that 6 of 7 rows are solved by adding one fortified staple to the weekly rotation plus standard fortified-staple shopping, if the diet includes fish and dairy. The pattern's strongest evidence lies in the global population data: several of the longest-living cohorts sit closer to the plant-forward end of this spectrum than to any modern all-vegetable ideology.

## Who it suits

- **Households where one partner is enthusiastic about plants** — every listed pattern has a couple-friendly edition, and the shared-kitchen is the pattern's natural habitat.
- **People managing cholesterol or blood-pressure first** — the LDL and systolic effects are the best-documented non-drug benefits outside DASH.
- **Whatever dose you choose** — pescatarian, vegetarian, vegan or flexitarian, all meet the evidence criteria. The strictest is not the "most correct"; it is the one that requires more planning.
