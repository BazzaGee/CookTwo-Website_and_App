Open with the thesis plainly. The vegan diet — no animal products at all — is one of the best-investigated patterns in the cohort literature, and the debate about whether it is *healthier* than pescatarian or vegetarian is still live. What the data is consistent about are the pattern's core outcomes: better body weight, lower blood pressure, lower LDL cholesterol, and the biggest diabetes-risk reduction of the entire plant-based spectrum. It is also — like any pattern that eliminates a food family — the one with the longest list of nutrient requirements to plan around. Here is the data, including the non-negotiables.

## What the cohorts show

<StudyCard name="Orlich et al. — vegan all-cause mortality (JAMA Internal Medicine, 2013)" design="Prospective cohort, five patterns" participants="73,308 Adventists" duration="~5.8 years" finding="Vegans tracked an 15% lower all-cause mortality (HR 0.85) than omnivores, with cardiometabolic deaths significantly lower in all vegetarian arms — one of the closest-to-zero-meat patterns tested anywhere in a large cohort." url="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23836264/" />

<StudyCard name="Tonstad et al. — diabetes stepwise drop (Nutrition, Metabolism & Cardiovascular Diseases, 2013)" design="Prospective cohort, five patterns" participants="60,903 adults" duration="2 years" finding="Vegans carried a 49% lower prevalence of type 2 diabetes — the largest single effect in the entire spectrum, driven by body weight but not fully explained by it." url="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21983060/" />

<StudyCard name="Dinu et al. — vegan disease profile meta-analysis (Critical Reviews in Food Science and Nutrition, 2017)" design="Systematic review" participants="96 observational studies" duration="—" finding="Vegan patterns associated with ~18% lower overall cancer incidence, better body weight and cholesterol — and flagged bone fracture risk in vegans as a repeated finding where calcium and vitamin D are not added." url="https://doi.org/10.1080/10408398.2016.1138447" />

The line study of note worth naming is the EPIC-Oxford cohort, the largest European dataset of vegans (~10,000 of 60,310 people) — which found fracture risk in strict vegans consistently elevated in subgroups where calcium and vitamin D intake was low. That is the pattern's one real-world price to monitor.

## The nutrient plan that makes it work

Vegan nutrition is not loaded, but it is meticulous — the plan is a predictable, repeatable shopping list:

1. **B12 — the non-negotiable.** Supplement or fortified foods, reliably, no exceptions. Deficiency within 1–2 years is physiological, not ideological.
2. **Iron and zinc — pairing plant iron with vitamin C.** Lentils, tofu, chickpeas, with a citrus squeeze or a side of bell peppers; the zinc reads clearer with cashews and pumpkin seeds.
3. **Calcium — fortify or tofu.** Calcium-set tofu, fortified plant milk, almonds, figs — edibles pushing the requirement above the dailies when dairy is out.
4. **Omega-3.** ALA from flax and chia converts to EPA/DHA at single-digit yields; a once-daily algae oil fixes the gap cheaply.
5. **Vitamin D.** Sunlight, fortified plant milk, or a standard supplement — more complicated in northern winters.
6. **Iodine.** Occasional seaweed or iodized salt. Variable by region, influenced by pregnancy.

Three things, and the pattern is fully competitive with the omnivore nutrition piles observed in the Adventist data.

<Callout variant="warning" title="Pregnancy, children and strict veganism">
Most major nutrition bodies accept that a *well-planned* vegan diet is safe for pregnancy, breastfeeding, infants and teenagers — with the operative words "well planned", plus B12, iron, iodine, calcium and vitamin D as active requirements. The failed planning in each of those windows is much more dangerous than a more restrictive adherence, which is why our disclaimer is firm. Anyone feeding a child or carrying a pregnancy on a vegan diet should work with a registered dietitian, not a Facebook group.
</Callout>

## Who a vegan diet suits

- **Anyone with ethics as a hard priority** — where it is the only honest answer, this is the one that plans best with B12 discipline.
- **Anyone managing weight, cholesterol and blood sugar together** — vegan patterns deliver the best automated package of those three in the cohort literature.
- **Anyone who likes to cook** — poorly planned veganism is a shop-aisle problem; well-planned uses one chapter of ingredients.
