Intermittent fasting is the only entry on this map with no food list at all. It specifies *when* rather than *what*: eat inside a window (16:8 — 16 hours fasting, 8 hours eating — is the canonical shape; 18:6, 20:4 and 5:2 are the relatives), then let everything else close. Its claim to the table is not that it burns fat by sorcery — when matched carefully against daily calorie restriction the trials now agree: the weight loss is the same. Its real edge is operational. For people who find counting exhausting, a clock is often easier discipline than a calculator.

Where many fail is understanding what IF *doesn't* do: it is not a food-quality exchange program. You can eat nachos and juice inside the window and nothing magical happens, because the window only compresses the number of eating occasions — it does not abolish the physiology of what you eat. This hub's [food quality guide](/diets#quality) would grade the inside-window menu, because "which direction the hour goes" matters far more than to count it.

## What the trials show

<StudyCard name="Gabel et al. — the 16:8 pilot (Nutrition and Healthy Aging, 2018)" design="Single-arm pilot" participants="23 adults with obesity" duration="12 weeks" finding="A 10am-to-6pm window produced ~3% body-weight loss and ~7 mmHg systolic blood-pressure fall, with participants spontaneously eating ~350 fewer calories a day with no calorie counting anywhere in the protocol." url="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29951154/" />

<StudyCard name="Cienfuegos et al. — 4-hour vs 6-hour eating windows (Cell Metabolism, 2020)" design="Randomized controlled trial" participants="82 adults with obesity" duration="8 weeks" finding="Both 20:4 and 18:6 produced ~3% weight loss and reduced fasting insulin and HOMA-IR — with no advantage to the more extreme 4-hour window. Tight windows buy nothing beyond 6 hours and research is honest on that." url="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32589740/" />

<StudyCard name="Patikorn et al. — the umbrella review (JAMA Network Open, 2021)" design="Umbrella review of meta-analyses of RCTs" participants="11 RCTs, n = 130–772 each" duration="—" finding="Time-restricted eating matched continuous calorie restriction for weight loss (mean difference −0.1 kg, non-significant), with moderate-confidence evidence for lower body weight, fat mass, total cholesterol and LDL — the honest settlement of the IF hype cycle." url="https://doi.org/10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2021.36437" />

<StudyCard name="Cho et al. — intermittent fasting and type 2 diabetes (Reviews in Endocrine and Metabolic Disorders, 2023)" design="Systematic review" participants="—" duration="—" finding="IF reduced HbA1c by 0.2–0.5 points in prediabetes and T2D — with the review medical supervision explicit: hypoglycaemia risk rises with any medication dose, so the intervention belongs within endocrine supervision in T2D." url="https://doi.org/10.2174/1381612825666230721105142z" note="The T2D application is promising and increasingly delivered under supervision, not DIY." />

<Callout variant="tip" title="Why IF works, without magic">
The onset date of ketogenesis (~12 hours), which many forums hold out as the "magic range", is real but overstated. First, splitting 24 hours into a window produces — without counting — a reliable *spontaneous* calories-in reduction of 300 to 550/day across trial data. Second, metabolic switching and autophagy effects are real, and may explain some markers-free improvement; but weight scale still works on the same slope. Third, a clock has *adherence psychology* — hard edges are easier for some people than soft counting. The pattern's advantage is plausible in the trial data, and it does not require interpretation your body does not already support.
</Callout>

## The honest limitations

**Disordered eating history is a hard stop.** The pattern wraps restriction into a rule with a particular shape; for anyone with an Anorexia or bulimia-history tunnel, the literature — and the intuitive advice — say no.

**Type 1 diabetes: outright contraindication** without endocrinology; type 2 diabetes requires supervision and medication review. The trial record is silent about medication doses and can only generalise within specialists.

**Pregnancy and adolescence.** Both periods have higher energy and macronutrient requirements; fasting protocols run against growth or fetal development.

**Protein erosion in practice.** A common complaint we see among 16:8-adopters: the *shared lunch-only* meal has only one protein anchor, while coffee-and-fruit on the other side of the day depletes it. This is worth fighting, with the planning section below.

## Who it suits

- **People who dread counting.** One rule instead of twelve is a real usability advantage — the largest adherence shift the pattern has.
- **Skillless benefit-seekers with mid-life blood-sugar concerns.** 16:8 adds consistent insulin windows, produces mild HOMA-IR improvements in pilot data — with appropriate medical boundaries.
- **Couples**, as long as one partner keeps the protein anchor rare vs. the other's three meals — a typical CookTwo household case.
